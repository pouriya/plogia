**Artificial Intelligence and the Crisis of Definition**

What is a precise definition? In logic, a definition is considered precise if it is “inclusive and exclusive”. Inclusive means it takes in every instance of the thing we are defining and leaves none of them out; exclusive means it lets nothing else in.

Let us test this on something simple: *table*. We sit at a table every day and never fail to recognize one, so defining it should not be hard. Perhaps the first definition that comes to mind is: “A table is a flat surface with four legs.” But this definition is not inclusive, because it leaves out three-legged and one-legged tables; nor is it exclusive, because it takes in a four-legged chair as well.
We can improve the definition and say: “A table is a surface for putting things on.” But then a shelf, a shop counter and even the floor of a room count as tables too. If we proceed properly and without cheating, however much we patch the definition, sooner or later an instance turns up that breaks it and shows that our definition is not inclusive and exclusive.

This is the trouble we call “the crisis of definition”: we use words every day and more or less understand each other's speech, yet for most of them we cannot give an inclusive and exclusive definition.

The root of this crisis lies in the function of language itself. Everyday language took shape less to deliver a message precisely than to deliver it quickly and easily. That is why two features have emerged in it: ambiguity (one word with several meanings) and vagueness (a word with a slippery boundary of meaning). These two are not shortcomings of language but functional features that raise its efficiency; and the crisis of definition is the price of that very efficiency.

Ambiguity can be seen in a word like the Persian *shir* (شیر), which means milk, lion or tap, and vagueness in the boundary between *cup* and *bowl*. Picture a vessel whose mouth grows gradually wider; from some point on we no longer call it a cup but a bowl, yet no one can say exactly where that boundary lies. Sometimes even the same vessel takes another name depending on what is in it: if we pour coffee into it, it is a cup; if we pour soup, a bowl.

Besides, so far we have spoken only of things like a table, which we can see and touch. If we turn to something like *feeling*, the work becomes much harder. There we cannot dissect a feeling even as far as we did a table, let alone give an inclusive and exclusive definition of it.

This crisis shows itself clearly in the way large language models work, and in our relationship with them. The people who built and trained these models are, like you and me, caught in the crisis of definition. The data the models were trained on was also written mostly by people who were, and are, caught in this same crisis. Finally, we users who work with these tools are not exempt from this crisis either.

One of the most important reasons AI can write code with such capability and attention to detail is that programming languages are not caught in this crisis. These languages, like the language of mathematics, were built deliberately, and every word in them has one clear, single meaning. That is, ambiguity and vagueness, from which the crisis of definition arises, have no place in these languages to begin with.

Of course, the talk of coding was a short digression. You might ask: we humans have no inclusive and exclusive definitions in hand either, and still we understand each other's speech; so why should AI not understand? The answer is that we have a world, a body and experiences like one another's, which fill the gap between words; but most of what a language model has in hand is those very words.

For this reason, AI will never precisely grasp our writings and sayings, just as we too do not precisely grasp each other's speech. The crisis of definition is in language itself and will always remain with us, and AI, which has no world and no body like ours, is caught in it more than we are. Perhaps if models are trained on a more precise vocabulary, and we too use that same vocabulary in our conversations with them, things will improve; but this improvement will never do away with the crisis entirely.
