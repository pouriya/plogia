I have recently become sensitive to words.

I have come to realize that language, whether in conversation or in any other way of conveying information, does not always seek precision; ease of conveying matters more to it.

A clear example of this useful imprecision is the Greek word *logos* (λόγος), which has found its way into many of the words we use today. Throughout history this word has had many meanings: speech, word, counting and reckoning, ratio, reason, argument and cause are only some of them.

For example, *dialogue* comes from the Greek word *dialogos* (διάλογος).
More interesting still, *logic*, in English and the other European languages, comes from this same root; and more interesting than that, the word we use for it in Persian, *manteq* (منطق), is also built from *notq* (نطق), that is, speaking.
The suffix *-logy*, which we hear in words like *psychology*, comes from the Greek suffix *-logia* (‑λογία), which also belongs to the logos family.

The name of this channel belongs to the same family too: it comes from *logia* (λόγια), meaning “sayings”, which itself springs from *logos*.
All these Greek words spring from the verb *legō* (λέγω), which, before it meant “speaking”, meant “gathering” and “choosing”.
*Plogia* (Π. λόγια), meaning “the sayings of P”: a place for gathering the words that I, Pouriya, have chosen, with all the imprecision that language imposes on me.

But a saying is only half of speech; the other half belongs to the listener or the reader. I hope these sayings will one day become a dialogue: speech that travels through us.
